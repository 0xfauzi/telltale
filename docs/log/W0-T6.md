## WHAT WAS WRONG

**1. The sail asked for in the brief cannot fill 60 percent of the box by area.** The brief
says "one curved sail shape in ink filling roughly 60 percent of the box". A triangle whose
three corners are the corners of a square fills exactly 50 percent of it by area, and a
sail is a triangle with one edge bowed out. Adding the margin a mark needs and the space the
ribbons need on the left, the reachable area figure is about a third. Measured on the
shipped mark by counting ink pixels in the 512 px render: the sail's filled area is 35.5
percent of the canvas.

The reading that is both reachable and useful is the sail's bounding box, which is what
"how much of the box does the sail occupy" means to the eye. Measured: the sail spans x
135..472, which is 66.0 percent of the width, and y 28..483, which is 89.1 percent of the
height, so its bounding box covers 58.8 percent of the canvas. That is the 60 percent, and
the mark was enlarged twice to reach it. Both numbers are in this report rather than one,
because the two answers to "60 percent of what" differ by a factor of 1.7 and a later
reader deserves to know which one was met.

**2. The first sail read as a leaf, not a sail.** The first draft bowed the leading edge
outward by 28 units at its midpoint, on the theory that a sail under wind is a curve. Looked
at, the silhouette was a rounded blob with a point: a leaf or a shark fin. The fix was to
make the leading edge taut, which is what it actually is on a boat because it is attached to
a mast, and to move all the curvature to the trailing edge. Measured on the shipped path by
walking each curve in 1000 steps and taking the largest perpendicular distance from its
chord: the leading edge bows 13.6 units and the trailing edge bows 36.1, a ratio of 2.7 to
1. Sailmakers call that second curve the roach, and it is the thing that makes the
silhouette read as a sail rather than as a triangle.

**3. A 30 unit ribbon disappears at 32 px.** The mark is drawn in a 512 unit box, so a
32 px render divides every dimension by 16. A stroke of 30 units is 1.9 px, which after
antialiasing is a grey line, not a red one. The ribbons were redrawn at 40 units, which is
2.5 px. Measured by decoding both 32 px renders and counting: fully coloured ribbon pixels
go from 31 to 46, an increase of 48 percent, while the number of half-lit pixels stays at
17. The two pixel maps are in the owner section below.

**4. `qlmanage` crops a non-square SVG instead of scaling it.** `qlmanage -t -s 512` on
`wordmark.svg`, which is 1600 by 512, produced a 512 by 512 PNG containing the mark and the
first two letters of the word. It scales to the height and crops the width, so it cannot
show a wide asset. The wordmark was checked by generating a scratch copy with a 1600 by
1600 viewBox and the content translated down by 544, rendering that at 1600, and measuring
the result. This is why the wordmark is verified but has no committed PNG: the tool that
rasterizes here cannot produce one at the right aspect ratio.

**5. `telltale purge` has no wave.** `SECURITY.md` has to say how a capture is deleted, and
the brief says to name `telltale purge <capture>`. The command is specified in
`docs/design/01-design.md` sections 6.5 and 6.13, but the brief's list of which wave ships
which command does not include it, and the house rule forbids inventing one. `SECURITY.md`
therefore names the command, cites the design sections that specify it, states plainly that
it is not implemented and that no brief has assigned it a wave, and tells the reader that
deleting the SQLite file under `$TELLTALE_HOME` is what works today.

## EXPLAIN FOR THE OWNER

### Why this task existed at all

The repository goes public one day. On that day, the first thing anybody sees is the README,
and the second is whether the project looks like it was built by somebody careful. That
judgement is made in about four seconds and it is mostly made on things that have nothing to
do with the code: is there a mark, do the badges render, is there a license, does the
quickstart work. This task front-loads all of that so the flip is a switch rather than a
project.

### The mark, and how it was decided

Start with the name. A telltale is a short length of yarn a sailor tapes to a sail. You
cannot see the wind, so you read the yarn instead: if it streams flat, the air is flowing
along the sail; if it flutters or droops, the flow has broken away. It is a cheap indirect
instrument for something you cannot observe directly. That is exactly what this project
does with a coding agent, so the mark is that object: a sail with three telltales on it.

Two colours only, because a mark that needs three is a mark that will look wrong the first
time somebody prints it in one. They are:

- **harbour ink `#16232F`**, a very dark blue-black, for the sail.
- **signal red `#D93A2B`**, for the three ribbons.

Red is not decoration. On a boat the port-side telltales are red, so red yarn on a sail is
the literal object, not a brand choice. It is also the one part of the mark that must stay
put when the background changes, because it is what the mark is about.

Colour choices like this are usually argued about. This one was measured. There is a
standard number for "can you see this shape against that background", the contrast ratio,
which runs from 1 to 1 (invisible) to 21 to 1 (black on white), and the accessibility
threshold for a graphical element is 3 to 1. Seven candidate reds were scored against all
four backgrounds each one has to survive: white, GitHub's dark page `#0D1117`, the light
sail, and the dark sail. `#D93A2B` was the only candidate above 3.4 to 1 on every one of
them, at 4.57, 4.14, 3.88 and 3.49. The nearest rival, a warmer orange-red `#E4572E`, drops
to 3.13 to 1 against the light sail, which is the join where a ribbon touches the sail in
dark mode, so it was rejected.

The dark variant changes exactly one value. The sail becomes **sailcloth `#E8EDF2`**, a
near-white with a trace of blue, which sits at 16.07 to 1 on GitHub's dark background. The
red does not move. A reader who sees the mark on both a light and a dark page sees the same
object lit differently, which is the point of having a variant at all rather than two logos.

### What the 32 px render showed, and what changed because of it

A logo is judged at two sizes: large, where you decide if you like it, and tiny, where you
find out if it works. The tiny size is a browser tab, which is 32 px across. The mark is
drawn in a 512 unit grid, so a 32 px render divides every measurement by 16.

The way to check this is not to squint at a thumbnail. The 32 px PNG was decoded pixel by
pixel and printed as a character grid, one character per pixel: `#` for sail, `R` for a
fully coloured ribbon pixel, `r` for a partly coloured one, `.` for nothing. Here is the
top ribbon with a 30 unit stroke, which is what the first drafts used:

```
 7 .........rrr......+#####+.......
 8 ........rRRRRRrrrr#######r......
 9 ........rrrRRRRRR########+......
10 .............rrrr#########r.....
```

A 30 unit stroke is 1.9 px, and 1.9 does not divide into whole pixels. The result is what
rows 8 and 9 show: the solid core is 5 pixels long on one row and 6 on the next, and they
are offset from each other, with the rest of the ribbon spent on half-lit fringe. Here are
the same rows with the shipped 40 unit stroke, which is 2.5 px:

```
 7 ........rrrrrrr...+#####+.......
 8 ........rRRRRRRRrr#######r......
 9 ........rRRRRRRRR########+......
10 .........rrrrrrrr#########r.....
```

Now there is a solid 7 and 8 pixel core, aligned, with one fringe row above and one below.
Counted over the whole 32 px image: fully coloured ribbon pixels went from 31 to 46, an
increase of 48 percent, while the number of half-lit pixels stayed at 17. That is the
measurement that decided the stroke width, and both grids were produced by rendering the
real files rather than by reasoning about them.

The whole mark was also enlarged so it uses more of the box, and the three ribbons keep
clear gaps: their roots sit 101 and 98 units apart, which at 32 px is 6.3 and 6.1 px, so
with a 2.5 px stroke the gaps are 3.8 and 3.6 px. Wide enough that they do not merge into
a red smear, which the grid above confirms: rows 11 and 12 are empty of red.

Two other things the 32 px look decided. The sail's leading edge had to be taut rather than
curved, because a bowed leading edge plus antialiasing turns the silhouette into a blob, and
a blob at 32 px is unidentifiable. And the mark had to grow, because the same 1 px of margin
costs 16 units of drawing at this scale: measured at 32 px, the mark went from 84.4 percent
of the frame's width and 90.6 percent of its height to 87.5 and 93.8. That is one pixel of
margin recovered on each side, which does not sound like much until you notice it is one
sixteenth of the whole design.

### Which badges render, and which one does not

Badges are images fetched by whoever is reading the page, using their credentials, not
yours. That matters while the repository is private. Every badge URL was fetched with
`curl -sI` and no authentication, which is exactly the request a stranger's browser makes:

The CI badge is the only one that asks GitHub a question about this repository, and GitHub
answers 404 to anybody who cannot see the repository. So today the badge row shows six
badges to you and six badges to a stranger who somehow reaches the page, with one broken
image where CI should be. The moment the repository goes public it answers 200 and the row
completes itself. Nothing needs changing at the flip; this is just what to expect if you
look at the page while logged out.

The six static badges are all set to the ink colour `#16232F`, so the row reads as one
object rather than seven, and it is made of the same two colours as the logo.

### The one thing you have to do by hand

**Upload `docs/assets/logo-512.png` as the repository's social preview.** This is the image
that appears when somebody pastes a link to the repository into Slack, Twitter or a
Discord channel. There is no API and no `gh` command for it, so it cannot be scripted:

1. Go to `https://github.com/0xfauzi/telltale/settings`.
2. Scroll to **Social preview**.
3. Click **Edit**, then **Upload an image**, and choose `docs/assets/logo-512.png` from the
   repository checkout.

One caveat measured rather than assumed: the file is 512 by 512 with a transparent
background, and GitHub's social preview card is a wide rectangle. It will centre a square
image and fill the rest, and a transparent background will be filled with whatever the
consuming site uses. If you want tighter control, the thing to ask for is a purpose-made
1280 by 640 image with an opaque background and the wordmark rather than the mark, which
this task did not produce because the tool that rasterizes SVG on this machine,
`qlmanage`, crops non-square art instead of scaling it.

### One thing worth knowing about the README's rules

The README shows five commands. Two of them run today. The other three do not exist yet,
and each one sits under an HTML comment naming the wave that ships it, so the source of the
README records the promise even though a reader of the rendered page does not see the
comment. That rule exists because a quickstart that lists commands nobody has written is
the single fastest way to lose a reader's trust, and it is a very easy mistake to make when
the design document lists twenty commands and the code implements two.
