put through `_approvable` by hand before the run. Recorded, not fixed: `experiments_env.py`
is not this task's file, and the fix is a decision about where the launch-safety check
belongs.

**`environment.json` was written without a trailing newline, so a committed one never

## UNSURE OR NOT DONE

- **One task, and a small one.** This task admits one solution shape, and twenty measures
  with no variation and no shift is a fact about ten samples of THIS task. A task with
  more choices in it has not been measured.
- **One model, one repository, one day, four minutes.** The other H3 factor design 6.12
  names, the model, has not been run. That is a new experiment id and a new spend.
- **What the effort flag does inside a turn is not observed.** The token counters moved and
  the fingerprint carries the value; nothing here watched the binary consume it. Claude
  Code 2.1.258 accepts low, medium, high, xhigh and max and WARNS on anything else while

# EXPLAIN FOR THE OWNER

## What a factor effect is

Every number this system reports moves on its own. Run the same task five times, changing
nothing, and the token counts come back slightly different each time. E05 measured that
wobble and called it the floor.

A factor is one thing you deliberately change. Here it is a single word on the command
line: `--effort low` against `--effort high`. A factor effect is the question of whether
changing that word moves a number further than the wobble does.

The reason we cannot just look at the two averages and see which is bigger is that the
bigger one is always bigger by something. Five sessions of anything have a highest and a
lowest. The question is whether the gap between the arms is large compared to the gap
inside each arm, and that comparison is the whole rule.

## What a changepoint is

Later on, this system wants to forecast: given the last forty rows of a session's
history, what does the next one look like? That only works if the rows are the same kind
of thing. If you upgrade the model half way through, or switch the effort setting, then
the rows before and after are measurements of two different machines, and a forecaster
trained straight across the join learns a blend of two things that never existed.

A changepoint is the row where the environment changed. Design 6.12 says a forecaster
must not train across one, and must not be scored on a window that straddles one.

The connection to this experiment is direct. We do not want to declare a changepoint every
time any flag moves, because that throws away half the data. So the rule is: a
fingerprint change is a changepoint FOR A MEASURE only if we measured that measure moving
further than the floor. This experiment is where that gets decided, and the answer here is
three measures out of twenty-eight, all of them token counts.

## One measure's two arms, computed by hand

Take `context_token_burden.output_tokens`, the tokens the model produced. Sorted, five
values in each arm:

```
low:  455, 466, 466, 468, 469
high: 467, 492, 575, 590, 631
```

The low arm's median is the middle value, 466. Its distances from that median are 11, 0,
0, 2, 3; the median of those distances is 2, and multiplying by 1.4826, the constant that
makes a median absolute deviation comparable to a standard deviation, gives a spread of

```
s_low = 1.4826 * 2 = 2.965
```

The high arm's median is 575. Its distances are 108, 83, 0, 15, 56; the median of those is
56, so

```
s_high = 1.4826 * 56 = 83.026
```

The two arms are pooled by taking the root mean square of the two spreads, which is what
design 6.12's `s` is for a two-arm comparison:

```
s = sqrt((2.965^2 + 83.026^2) / 2) = sqrt((8.79 + 6893.3) / 2) = sqrt(3451.0) = 58.745
```

Now the smallest difference five-against-five can separate from run-to-run variation:

```
MDD = 2.8 * 58.745 * sqrt(2/5) = 2.8 * 58.745 * 0.632456 = 104.03 tokens
```

And the shift itself. The Hodges-Lehmann shift is the median of all 25 differences you get
by subtracting each low value from each high value. It is not the difference of the
medians, though it usually lands near it; it is robust in the same way the spread is, and
here it comes to 109 tokens.

The rule then reads:

```
|HL shift| = 109 > MDD = 104.03
```

109 is larger than 104.03, so this measure takes the material branch: the effort
fingerprint is a changepoint for `output_tokens` on this task. Notice how close it is. The
shift clears the bar by five tokens. That is a verdict a couple more sessions could easily
have turned over, and it is exactly why the write-up prints the inequality with both of
its numbers rather than just the label.

The exact Mann-Whitney p for these two arms is 0.032, computed by counting all 252 ways of
splitting the ten values into two fives rather than by any approximation. Cliff's delta is
0.84, meaning that of the 25 low-against-high pairings, high is the larger one in 92 per
cent of them. Both agree with the verdict, and neither is what the rule reads: the rule
reads the inequality above.

## The difference between "not resolved at n = 5" and "no effect"

Four measures moved and did not clear their MDD. The write-up calls each of them "not
resolved at n = 5", never "no effect", and the distinction is the point of the whole
protocol.

Take `duration_ms`, how long the agent said it took. The shift is 646 ms and the MDD is
1463 ms. So the difference we measured is real in the sense that it is what the ten
sessions did, and it is smaller than what ten sessions can tell apart from noise. Three
different things are consistent with that, and this experiment cannot separate them:
there is no difference; there is a difference smaller than 1463 ms; or there is a large
difference that ten sessions happened to sample badly.

"No effect" claims the first of those three. We have not earned it, and we cannot earn it
by running the experiment we ran. What we can say is "this experiment, at five per arm,
cannot separate a shift of this size from run-to-run variation for this measure", and the
phrase "not resolved at n = 5" is the compressed form of that sentence with the n left in
so nobody forgets what it depends on.

The practical difference matters downstream. If a report said "effort has no effect on
duration", somebody later models a series straight across an effort change and gets a
forecast built on an assumption nobody ever tested. Saying "not resolved at n = 5" keeps
the question open and names its price: the report also prints `N_needed`, the number of
sessions per side it takes to settle it. For `duration_ms` that is 2 per arm, which is
below the 5 already run, and that tells you something sharper than the label alone: more
sessions are not what this row is short of. The shift really is smaller than the spread.

Contrast `final_diff_lines`, where the low arm wrote two lines every single time and the
high arm wrote 2, 2, 6, 6 and 9. That looks like a real difference and it reads "not
resolved at n = 5", with `N_needed` 621 per arm. Design 6.12 caps scaling at 20, so the
honest reading is that this measure is never going to settle this question. The thing that
does separate the two arms is the hash of the patch itself: one patch five times on the
low side, three different patches on the high side. It is in the recorder, it is not in
the measures, and that gap is now written down twice.
