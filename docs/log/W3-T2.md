## WHAT WAS WRONG

**Design 6.12's own suggestion for the "conditional prediction" fixture does not clear
delta, and this was measured before anything was written.** The brief names the mechanism:
a stub whose forecast is a function of the multiset of its context, such as the
full-context mean, on an i.i.d. noise series where the window-8 baselines lose to it. The
first half is right and load-bearing; the second half is not true at delta 0.10. Measured
over 13 distribution families (normal, uniform, exponential, lognormal at sigma 0.5, 1.0,
1.5, 2.0, gamma at shape 0.3, 0.5, 1.0, Pareto at alpha 1.5, 2.0, 3.0), 8 seeds each, 400
rows, c_min 32, comparing the full-context mean and the full-context median against the
best of the four baselines:

| family | best stub ratio to best baseline (mean over 8 seeds) | best W_MB |
|---|---|---|
| normal | 0.943 | 0.551 |
| uniform | 0.973 | 0.529 |
| exponential | 0.939 | 0.549 |
| lognormal 1.0 | 0.940 | 0.563 |
| gamma 0.5 | **0.932** | 0.582 |
| gamma 0.3 | 0.934 | **0.585** |
| Pareto 2.0 | 0.952 | 0.547 |

Not one family reached the 0.900 the first clause needs, and not one reached W_MB 0.60. The
reason is structural rather than a matter of picking a better distribution: on i.i.d. data
every baseline is estimating the same constant, and the whole gain available is the gain
from 8 samples to 400, which for MAE is about 6 percent. Such a series is labelled
"baseline sufficient", correctly, and contract test 3(b) would have been asserting the
wrong label.

The fix keeps the essential half of the mechanism and drops the accidental half. The stub
still reads only the MULTISET of its context, so E_P is E_M to the last bit and the
temporal branch is unreachable by construction; but it fits a least-squares line to the
SORTED context, and the series is a noisy straight line rather than noise. Sorting a
monotone series recovers its order, so the stub is as good as an order-aware method while
being provably order-blind. Measured: E_M 2.5263 against E_B 3.9610 (local_drift), a ratio
of 0.638, W_MB 0.6978, E_P exactly E_M, W_MP 0.0. Label: conditional prediction.

**The change-clock fixture had no recency, so its placebo was void by design 6.12's own
rule.** The first version of `synthetic_series.write_change` drew every row independently.
Persistence then scored 0.7045 in true order and was worse in only 7 of 10 placebo runs,
because on independent draws the row a permutation leaves last is as good as the row that
was there. The ablation could not be labelled at all. The size of a change now walks, for
the reason the request-clock walk already gives in its own docstring, and persistence goes
0.4773 to between 1.11 and 1.84 across all ten runs. This is the machinery working: the
validity check caught a fixture that could not have tested what it was for.

**Two strings in this task's own code tripped the word refusal.** The reason string for a
withheld label read "calling it either way would be a claim about order", and the
candidate refusal read "would be scoring a lookup". Both are printed or raised by this
package, so both were rewritten. The check found them within a minute of existing.

**Three facts in the brief's injected notes did not match the code**, and the code is what
was used. `backtest.run` has the signature `run(series, target, horizon, forecasters,
variant=None, *, c_min=None)` rather than `run(store, series, target, variant,
forecasters, ordering="true")`: it takes no store, which W1-T6's docstring already
explains as its one deviation. backtest.py was 727 lines with a hard ceiling of 800 rather
than a soft one. And `readiness.py` already carried its own `PLACEBO_BLOCK_MIN = 2`, so
that constant now exists in two modules; readiness.py is not in this task's OWNS and was
left alone.

## UNSURE OR NOT DONE

  covariate-reading forecaster.** The four baselines read only the target column and the
  stub reads only its last value, so on any series A, B and C score identically and the

  own numbers and warn, so no reader can take the verdict for a statement about the

# EXPLAIN FOR THE OWNER

## The problem, in thirty seconds

A forecast that should never have been believed does not look wrong. It comes back in the
right units, with a plausible band around it, and the only thing the matter with it is that
nothing in the data supported it. Wave 1 and wave 2 built the machinery that scores a
forecaster honestly against four crude rules. What that machinery could not do is tell you
WHY a forecaster won, and the answer everybody assumes is the one thing it never tested.

This task adds the test, the rule that reads it, and a page teaching you to read both:
`docs/design/04-forecast-reading-guide.md`.

## What a placebo is here

Borrow the idea from medicine, where it is older and clearer. A drug trial that gives the
drug to a hundred people and watches them get better has learned something, but not what it
set out to learn: people get better on their own, and they get better when somebody pays
attention to them. The placebo arm exists to hold everything constant except the one thing
under test.

A forecast has the same problem, in a shape that is easy to miss. Suppose a model forecasts
next request's token count better than "say whatever it was last time". You will read that
as "the model learned how a session evolves". But there are other explanations. Maybe it
learned the typical SIZE of a request in this session, which is a fact about the
distribution and not about the sequence. Maybe it learned that this column tracks another
column. Neither of those is knowledge about time, and both would produce the same table.

So: run the identical backtest again with the past scrambled. If the model was reading the
ORDER of the rows, scrambling destroys what it was reading and its score collapses. If it
was reading the level or the distribution, scrambling changes nothing, because a permuted
history has the same level and the same distribution.

## Why shuffling the past is the honest control, and what "honest" costs

The scramble has to destroy the order and nothing else. Three properties get that, and each
one was a decision.

**Whole rows move together.** A row of the request clock is one model request: its fresh
tokens, its cached tokens, its output tokens, its tool calls, all measured at the same
moment. If the columns were shuffled independently, the scramble would destroy the order
AND the fact that these values belong together, and a collapse afterwards could not be
attributed to either. So the context is cut into consecutive blocks of two whole rows and
the blocks are permuted. Every row still holds exactly the values it held, and a covariate
still sits beside the target value it was measured with. The MULTISET of rows is preserved
exactly; only their order is gone.

**Only the past moves.** The origin, the row being forecast and its true value are
untouched. This is the part that would be easiest to get wrong and hardest to notice: if
the row being forecast also moved, the placebo run would be answering a different question
at a different place in the history, and comparing it with the true-order run would be
comparing two unrelated experiments. Because only the context moves, every placebo window
has a twin at the same origin with the same right answer, and you can ask "did the true
order beat its own twin?" origin by origin rather than only on the average. In the code
this is guaranteed by construction rather than by care: the backtester writes down the
window record from the true rows first, and only then hands the window to the thing that
scrambles it.

**The baselines are scrambled too, and one of them is the alarm.** Persistence is "say
whatever happened last time", so it reads exactly one row of the past: the last one. If the
scramble reached the forecasters at all, it moved that row. So persistence MUST get worse.
If it does not, one of two things is true: the scramble never arrived (a bug), or the
series has no recency in it to destroy (a fixture that cannot test what it was built for).
Either way the control controlled for nothing, and the run is void. This is not a nicety.
It caught a real defect in this task: the first version of the synthetic change series drew
every row independently, persistence came out worse in only 7 of 10 placebo runs, and the
run was correctly refused a label. On the fixed fixture it is 10 of 10.

What the honesty costs is that the placebo is not free evidence. It is 10 extra backtests
per pair, and it can come back saying "I cannot tell you anything", which is the answer a
system that will not lie to you has to be able to give.

## The four labels, with the real numbers

Five numbers go into the rule. E_M is the model's average error in true order. E_B is the
best of the four baselines. E_P is the model's average error on the scrambled contexts,
taken as the median over five seeds. W_MB is the share of origins where the model beat the
best baseline. W_MP is the share where the true-order model beat its own scrambled twin.
Two thresholds were fixed before any result existed: a margin `delta = 0.10` and a share
`w = 0.60`.

### baseline sufficient

The synthetic 200-row walk, forecasting fresh input tokens, with the registry's stub as the
model:

```
E_M 90.4338   E_B 90.4338 (persistence)   W_MB 0.5
E_M > (1 - delta) E_B    90.4338 vs 81.3904   holds
W_MB < w                 0.5000  vs 0.6000    holds
```

Either clause alone is enough. The first says the model did not beat the best crude rule by
a wide enough margin; the second says it did not beat it often enough, which catches the
model that wins on average by winning enormously on three origins out of a hundred. A
single average cannot tell those two apart, which is why the rule asks both.

What this label lets you say: on this series, at this horizon, a one-line rule did as well.
What it does not let you say: that the model is bad, or that the series is unforecastable.
You tested one model, one variant, one horizon.

Note that this branch needs no placebo at all, which is why E07 could label 19 pairs before
this task existed.

### not assessable

200 rows of independent draws, forecast by a stub that adds 400 tokens to the last value.
Persistence scored about the same either way and only 3 of 10 placebo runs came out worse.
The report prints `3 of 10 placebo runs worse -> INVALID` and writes no label, although the
baseline clause on its own would have fired with room to spare (E_M 401.14 against a best
baseline of 90.06). A run whose control failed licenses nothing at all, including the
comfortable conclusion.

### temporal evolution, and conditional prediction

These two are the point of the whole exercise, and the clearest way to see the difference
is a pair of forecasters that a backtest table cannot tell apart.

The series is a noisy straight line: each value is ten more than the last, plus a small
random wobble. Both forecasters fit a least-squares line to the context and step one row
past the end of it. They differ in one thing. One sorts the context first.

Sorting a rising series very nearly puts it back in order, so the two fit almost the same
line and score almost the same:

```
                    sorted first        true order
E_M                 2.5263              2.5264      tokens
E_B                 3.9610              3.9610      (local_drift, both)
W_MB                0.6978              0.6978
```

Identical to three decimals, identically ranked against the baselines, identically not
baseline sufficient. Now the placebo:

```
E_P                 2.5263              788.6690
W_MP                0.0000              1.0000
label               conditional         temporal evolution
                    prediction
```

The sorted one does not move at all. It cannot: its answer is a function of the sorted
values, and a permutation of whole rows does not change which values are there. So E_P is
E_M to the last bit, the test `E_M <= 0.9 E_P` reads `x <= 0.9x`, and no positive number
satisfies it. The unsorted one collapses from 2.53 to 789, and beats its own twin at every
single origin.

So: **temporal evolution** means the model's advantage over the baselines depends on the
order of the rows, and **conditional prediction** means it does not. The first licenses you
to say the model is using the sequence. The second licenses you to say the model is reading
something real in the window that the baselines miss, and it is not the sequence. Where the
variant carries no other columns at all, there is nothing left for it to be reading but the
level of the target itself, and the report says so in those words: "level prediction: the
gain is distributional".

A reader given only the backtest table could not have told these two apart. That is what
the placebo buys.

### The clause that stops a result being about half a history

Temporal evolution has one more condition, and it earns its place. The two inequalities
must hold separately in the first half of the origins and in the second half.

Here is why, on a fixture built for it: a ramp for 150 rows, then independent draws.

```
pooled over 236 origins:
  E_M <= (1 - delta) E_P   1.6970 vs 206.1989   holds
  W_MP >= w                0.7203 vs 0.6000     holds
first  half [32, 149]:  E_M 2.5474  E_P 457.3595  W_MP 1.0000   both hold
second half [182, 299]: E_M 0.8466  E_P   0.8429  W_MP 0.4407   DOES NOT HOLD
```

Pooled, both inequalities pass comfortably. They pass because the ramp half carries them:
in the second half the model does not beat its own scrambled twin at all. Without the half
clause this is labelled "temporal evolution", which reads as a claim about the whole
history and would be false about half of it. With the clause it drops to "conditional
prediction", which is true. Deleting the clause from the code makes the test
`test_the_half_clause_refuses_a_label_the_whole_range_earns` fail with
`assert 'temporal evolution' == 'conditional prediction'`, which is how I know the clause
is load-bearing rather than decorative.

## Why "would" is refused

Three words cannot appear anywhere in a forecast report: **cause**, **impact**, **would**.
The renderer checks the finished text and raises before anything is printed or stored, so a
report that uses one is never produced rather than produced and regretted.

The reason is the shape of the evidence, and it is worth being precise about. A backtest
observes ONE history. It can say, honestly and usefully, what a rule scored on rows it had
not been shown. "Cause" and "impact" claim that changing one thing changes another, and
"would" claims what the branch we did not observe holds. There is no such branch in the
data. There is no world in this store where the model was not switched, the session did not
compact, the change was not merged. The number that measures a difference between two
FORECASTS is one thing; the number that would measure a difference between two OUTCOMES
does not exist, because only one outcome was ever observed.

This bites hardest exactly where it is most tempting: the candidate protocol. It forecasts
what happens after a change is merged, twice: once knowing nothing about the change, once
knowing its diff statistics. The difference between the two is a genuinely interesting
number, and it is not the effect of merging that change. So every candidate output carries
one mandatory sentence:

> The difference between the conditioned and unconditioned forecast measures how much the
> candidate's known features change the forecast; it is not the effect of merging the
> candidate, because only one future is observed.

That sentence contains the word "because", which contains the letters of "cause". The check
matches whole words, so it passes, and there is a test asserting both halves of that: the
sentence passes and a report saying "would" raises. The rule is not something a report can
argue with. It can only be answered by a different kind of evidence, which is the point:
every forecast row in this database carries the claim class `predictive`, the weakest of
the four, and nothing anywhere may present a number as stronger evidence than the layer
that produced it.

## The one thing to be sceptical of in this task

The ablation and the candidate protocol both ran, both stored their rows, and both produced
verdicts that mean less than they look like they mean. On the change series A, B and C
scored 1.0682 each, and the conditioned and unconditioned candidate runs differed by
exactly 0. The reason is not subtle: none of the five forecasters available today reads a
covariate at all. The four baselines read the target column, the stub reads its last value.

I could have hidden that behind a verdict. Instead both runners read their own numbers and
say it, in the report, above the verdict: "A, B and C scored identically: no forecaster in
this run read a covariate at all, so the verdict is a property of the forecasters and not
of the columns." It is derived from the data rather than from a capability somebody
declared, so it disappears on its own the day the numbers stop being equal. Until a
covariate-reading forecaster runs, the honest reading of those two tables is that the
protocol works and the question has not been asked yet.
